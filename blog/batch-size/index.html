The Best Optimizer Depends on Batch Size

  • Xingyu Dang†Princeton Universityxd7812@cs.princeton.edu
  • Kaiyue Wen†Stanford Universitykaiyuew@stanford.edu
  • Sadhika MalladiUniversity of California, San Diegosamalladi@ucsd.edu

Read the paper ↗

Larger batches can speed up training, but often require a different learning rate and other hyperparameters.1 Scaling rules tell us how to adjust these settings without another search. But different theories recommend different rules for the same optimizer.

Q1Can a hyperparameter scaling rule consistently work in different training settings?

Batch size may also change which optimizer works best. Larger batches reduce gradient noise, which can affect optimizers differently. However, standard optimization benchmarks like AlgoPerf and NanoGPT rank optimizers according to experiments run at a fixed batch size. We compare optimizers across batch sizes and carefully retune each one at every batch size.

Q2Do optimizer rankings persist across batch sizes after extensive retuning?

TL;DR

  1. The best scaling rule depends on the task and target batch size.

  2. Optimizer rankings can reverse as batch size changes, even after extensive hyperparameter retuning.

  3. In a SignSGD toy model, learning rate scaling depends on curvature relative to noise.

  4. On a noisy quadratic, SGD can win at small batches and Newton at large batches, even after optimal retuning.

  5. In language-model pretraining, keeping small-batch updates in the sharpest 0.001% of matrix directions removes up to 59.5% of the error from scaling the batch size.

In these experiments, Muon updates matrix weights and AdamW updates auxiliary parameters.2 The equations below show how each rule adjusts Muon’s hyperparameters when the batch size grows by a factor of κ. Primes denote their values at the new batch size.

Matching an SDE

Linear learning rate.

ηM′=κηM,μ′=μκ,λM′=λM

We adapt an SDE-matching framework for Adam and RMSprop to Muon.3 The derivation holds the gradient–momentum mixing coefficient fixed, but Muon ties it to momentum, so the match is not exact.

Minimizing a bound

Square-root learning rate.

ηM′=κηM,μ′=μ,λM′=λM

This approach balances two terms in a theoretical convergence bound: one for initialization and one for loss smoothness.4 The square-root rule applies until the bound’s effective step-size cap is reached.

Power Lines

Fixed learning rate.

ηM′=ηM,μ′=μ,λM′=κλM

Power Lines keeps AdamW’s nominal parameter-averaging timescale fixed as batch size changes at a fixed token budget.5 We apply the same identity to Muon while holding momentum fixed.

We tune this configuration at a reference batch size for language modeling and image classification, then increase the batch size while keeping each task’s training budget fixed. We compare hundreds of scaling rules, including the candidates above and other combinations of learning rate, momentum, and weight-decay adjustments.6

Takeaway: The best scaling rule depends on both the task and target batch size.

Takeaway: Newton can reduce bias (remaining initial error) at the cost of higher variance (fluctuations from noisy gradients). Larger batches reduce the variance, which can make Newton beat SGD.

We tune each optimizer’s learning rate within its stable range to minimize expected loss after 4,096 samples. The animation shows individual runs, which can fluctuate near the minimum because of gradient noise.

For every sufficiently large sample budget, there exists a two-dimensional NQM on which optimally tuned SGD outperforms optimally tuned Newton at batch 1, but the ranking reverses when the whole budget forms one batch. The theorem holds both without momentum and with optimally tuned momentum. The reversal comes from a bias–variance tradeoff: Newton’s preconditioning can reduce initialization error faster at the cost of greater variance from gradient noise. Larger batches reduce the noise contribution, changing the balance between the two optimizers. We next examine how this tradeoff varies across directions and affects their learning rate scaling rules.

Takeaway: Even for the same optimizer, the best scaling rule depends on the landscape and the gradient noise.

Protocol

In the noisy quadratic setting, ideal Muon orthogonalization on a 1 × 1 block is the sign operation, motivating this SignSGD comparison. The ratio is CNR=h2c.

Momentum is fixed at μ=0.9. Curvature is h=1, single-example noise variance is c=1/CNR, initialization is w0∼N(0,1), and initial momentum is zero. The recursion applies the sign of momentum before each parameter update, without weight decay. For each batch, the search minimizes expected terminal loss after the fixed 4,096-sample budget.

The paper uses 121 broad logarithmic learning rate candidates with 512 paths, refines three separated neighborhoods, selects with 2,048 independent paths, and evaluates on eight held-out groups of 1,024 paths. The fitted exponent is an ordinary least-squares slope of log learning rate against log batch over the nine tested batches. It summarizes those endpoints rather than asserting a universal power law.

The playable adds 325 independently tuned toy-model points, giving 22 CNR conditions and 16 batches. The 27 paper measurements and their fitted exponents remain unchanged. Each slider position selects an actually tuned CNR; lines connect measured points. Additional batches use floor(4,096/B′) updates, with actual sample counts recorded in the dataset. Supplemental tuning follows the same search and independent selection protocol, then evaluates the chosen rate on eight held-out groups.

Takeaway: One learning rate scaling rule cannot preserve movement in both directions as batch size grows.

The two SignSGD curves respond differently because:

  1. When noise often flips the sign, larger batches help SignSGD choose the right direction. Roughly square-root learning rate scaling keeps progress per sample similar.
  2. When the sign is already reliable, larger batches mainly mean fewer updates. Roughly linear learning rate scaling makes up for those missing steps.

Protocol

Throughout the blog, B is a batch size before scaling, B′ the scaled batch, and κ=B′B their ratio. η is a learning rate, μ a momentum coefficient, and α a learning rate exponent. The local curves hold both coordinates at w=1 and assume stationary momentum with μ=0.9.

Assume independent Gaussian gradient noise. Freeze the coordinate w and let the momentum distribution settle to its steady state; this is stationary momentum. Let μ be the momentum coefficient and κ=B′B the batch ratio. The expected sign is A(B)=erf(wCNR·B(1+μ)2(1−μ)). Relative movement per processed sample is κα−1A(κB)A(B). We use reference B=1. Noise-dominated response grows approximately as B; signal-dominated response saturates. Preserving this local movement motivates square-root or linear scaling in the respective limits. The approximation freezes the parameter and assumes stationary momentum; it does not guarantee full-trajectory invariance. See Section 5 and Appendix C of the paper.

The sharp-subspace intervention.

Does a small set of directions account for the cost of increasing batch size in a language model? Building on the previous checkpoint-branching method12, we test different batch sizes in different matrix directions.

We compare a 128K-token control with updates scaled to 2M tokens. In other branches, we keep small-batch updates in the sharpest directions or in a random subspace. We select sharp directions using a local curvature estimate after Muon’s preconditioning. All other parameters keep their original AdamW updates.

Branches start from the same checkpoint and use the same training data. Recovery measures a local loss penalty, not savings in total training cost.

Keeping small-batch updates in the sharpest 768 directions, about 0.001% of the matrix-parameter space, removes up to 59.5% of the local penalty. A random subspace of the same size changes it by at most 3%. The benefit largely disappears near the end of training.13

Takeaway: A tiny set of sharp directions can drive much of the local error from increasing batch size.

Protocol

These are the paper’s original held-out-loss curves: 1M-token evaluations every 32 base steps. The endpoints below use a separate 10.5M-token evaluation. Checkpoints 1K–11K continue for 1,024 base steps; the 12K checkpoint continues for 992 steps to the end of training. Top-128 was not run at 7K, 9K, or 11K.

The sharp directions have the largest eigenvalues of a Muon-preconditioned Gauss–Newton approximation to the loss Hessian, a local curvature proxy. Branches continue for 1,024 base steps, except the 12K checkpoint, which runs for 992 steps to the end of training. The penalty subtracts its small-batch control’s final validation loss.

The held subspace stays fixed throughout the continuation. Both Muon copies receive the same stream of base-batch gradients. Every 16 steps, the large-batch copy uses their mean gradient and 16 times their mean learning rate. Matrix weight decay and auxiliary AdamW updates stay on the base clock. This is a diagnostic intervention, not an ordinary large-batch training run.

Endpoints use 10.5M held-out tokens. Most branches are single runs, so these percentages are descriptive measurements, not significance estimates. The experiment selects directions by curvature and does not measure the full direction-dependent noise covariance. Its effect largely disappears near the end of training. We therefore cannot infer a 59.5% improvement in final loss, total training cost, or wall-clock speed.

Takeaways

Batch size can change which optimizer works best, even after retuning. We encourage researchers to design and test optimizers for the batch sizes they will actually be used with. Deriving optimizers and scaling rules that jointly consider gradient noise and the loss landscape is an important direction for future work.
